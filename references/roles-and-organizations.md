# Roles, markets, and organizational work

**Sources:** Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People: Tactics for Management and Company Building* (supplied first edition, copyright 2022), chs. 2–5; Brannick, Cadle & Levine, “Job Analysis for Knowledge, Skills, Abilities, and Other Characteristics, Predictor Measures, and Performance Outcomes,” in Neal Schmitt (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of Personnel Assessment and Selection* (2012), ch. 7. The market-positioning procedure and function examples below are advisor synthesis, not a labor-market survey or a taxonomy from either book.
**Trigger:** identifying plausible U.S. occupations, adjacent or bridge roles; interpreting a technology-company job; deciding whether further education closes a hiring gap; evaluating an employer's organizational environment.
**What this file is:** a procedure for relating demonstrated candidate capabilities to actual work, organization, and entry constraints. It does not replace the original study-pathway module or provide current vacancies.

> **Vintage note.** Job-analysis logic, role clarity, and organizational dependencies are durable. Named companies' structures, role titles, levels, remote-work terms, salaries, headcount, sponsorship policies, and hiring requirements require current evidence. The books' company examples are historical examples, not present employer policy.

## 1. Position a candidate in a market, not beside a title

A market hypothesis specifies **function × industry/problem domain × organization type/stage × seniority × geography/work arrangement**. “Technology” alone identifies neither an occupation nor the work. A lawyer may have relevant domain knowledge without the execution skills of a product manager; a policy title may denote research, external affairs, product rules, enforcement, or operations.

Use this sequence, scaled to the question:

1. **Candidate profile.** Establish demonstrated tasks, work products, scope of responsibility, domain expertise, languages, credentials, preferences, available time and money, location, and known authorization constraints. Distinguish a demonstrated capability from a self-description. Ask only for missing facts that could change the decision.
2. **Generate role families.** Include a direct continuation, one or more adjacent functions, and a bridge route. Treat them as hypotheses. Avoid narrowing everything to one admired company or the candidate's current professional title.
3. **Analyze actual work.** For each hypothesis, identify customers, recurring tasks, decisions, outputs, success criteria, tools, dependencies, and the expected autonomy at the relevant level. Use current job descriptions plus practitioner evidence when available; a posting is an employer's stated requirement, not proof of the work or of criterion validity.
4. **Separate constraints from preferences.** Record required credentials, work authorization, location and timing as verified, unresolved, or disqualifying. Do not turn an unknown into a rejection. Degree preference, prestige screening, demonstrated skill, and legal permission are different gates.
5. **Map evidence.** Match each material requirement to an owned example or artifact. Mark missing evidence separately from missing skill. Prioritize gaps that could reverse the ranking, not every keyword absent from a résumé.
6. **Compare routes.** Recommend immediate applications, a bounded experiment, a bridge role, or education only after identifying what the route would change. State the leading alternative, rejection reason, and the evidence that would move it back into consideration.

A useful comparison has columns: role hypothesis; actual work and level; candidate evidence; hard gates; uncertain assumptions; smallest discriminating test; decision condition. Do not manufacture a numeric “fit score” when the underlying measures are incomparable.

**Source boundary:** these sources help construct and investigate plausible markets. They do not establish how many jobs exist, which markets are growing now, how foreign credentials are received by a particular employer, or a candidate's hiring probability. Occupational descriptions and aggregate market evidence should be checked through current official occupational sources, then compared with dated employer-level evidence. Aggregate demand does not establish individual accessibility.

## 2. Use job analysis to unpack requirements

Schmitt ch. 7 treats job analysis as systematic discovery of work content and the **knowledge, skills, abilities, and other characteristics (KSAOs)** needed to perform it. Work-oriented analysis starts from duties and tasks; worker-oriented analysis identifies attributes needed to accomplish them. A competency label such as “strategic” is not yet a usable requirement.

For each requirement ask: **which task needs it, at what level, how often, with what consequence of failure, and what would count as evidence?** Distinguish what must be present on entry from what the organization can train. “Strong communication” might mean concise executive decisions, interviewing users, writing enforceable policies, or explaining technical constraints; evidence for one does not automatically cover the others.

The **Critical Incident Technique** elicits an actual situation, effective or ineffective behavior, and consequences from incumbents or supervisors. Adapt it for informational conversations: ask about a recent difficult decision, what good performance looked like, and what a new hire commonly gets wrong. A single incident is a clue to verify, not a complete job analysis.

A performance criterion also needs scrutiny. Revenue, case volume, manager satisfaction, quality, adaptability, and retention measure different outcomes. Results may reflect resources, territory, team support, or supervisor preferences. An interview criterion's use by an employer does not establish that it predicts the job's important outcomes. See [Recruitment and selection](recruitment-and-selection.md).

## 3. Read the organization behind the work

Hughes Johnson's **Foundations and Planning for Goals and Resources**, **A Comprehensive Hiring Approach**, **Intentional Team Development**, and **Feedback and Performance Mechanisms** supply a way to ask what environment a candidate would enter. They are practitioner frameworks from scaling companies, not a universal sequence every employer follows.

- **Founding documents and operating principles:** distinguish an advertised mission from principles used in difficult decisions. Ask for an example of a tradeoff, not another slogan.
- **Team Charter:** identify mission, vision, customers, metrics, risks, dependencies, and how others work with the team. An unavailable charter is not proof of dysfunction; reconstruct these questions from interviews and documents.
- **Operating system and operating cadence:** identify how annual plans, quarterly goals, progress reviews, and communication connect company priorities to team work. A goal's existence does not establish clear ownership or resources.
- **Team structures:** start from strategy and the allocation of people and authority. Functional expertise, product/region ownership, and cross-functional coordination impose different interfaces. Solid-line reporting, dotted-line influence, and final decision authority can differ.
- **Hiring and onboarding:** distinguish high-volume roles with established rubrics from uncommon or leadership roles whose definition is still being discovered. Ask about onboarding, a manager's availability, the initial work, and the threshold for independent ownership.
- **Feedback and performance:** ask how expectations are set, evidence is collected, and disagreement is handled. **Hypothesis-based coaching** treats an observation as a testable interpretation; it should not be mistaken for a diagnosis of a person's fixed traits.

For a candidate, the actionable questions are: Who owns the decision? What can this role change? Which team supplies essential inputs? Which metrics are controllable? What happens when legal risk, user needs, engineering capacity, and commercial goals conflict? What support exists for the capabilities the candidate is expected to learn?

## 4. Interpret technology functions through outputs

The following are **illustrative advisor hypotheses**, not claims that Hughes Johnson supplies an occupational taxonomy or that a particular employer uses these definitions. Confirm the actual posting and team context.

| Function | Work/output to investigate | Evidence to look for; boundary to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Product | User/problem discovery, prioritization, requirements, tradeoffs, outcome evaluation | A reasoned decision tied to user evidence and delivery constraints; enthusiasm for a product is insufficient |
| Operations / program operations | Reliable workflows, service quality, incident response, coordination, process improvement | A process with owners, metrics, exceptions, and evidence of improvement; organizing an event is not automatically operating a scaled system |
| Strategy / business operations | Framing choices, market or business analysis, resource allocation, executive recommendations | Assumptions, data, alternatives and a decision; legal argument alone does not show quantitative or commercial competence |
| Policy / trust and safety | Policy development, interpretation, implementation, enforcement feedback, stakeholder engagement | A clear rule and edge cases linked to operational consequences; external public policy and internal product policy can require different evidence |
| Legal / compliance | Legal analysis, advice, contracts, controls, investigations, regulatory coordination | Relevant legal or control work plus jurisdiction and authorization checks; a nonlegal role is not licensed practice by another name |
| Technical / engineering / data | Building or analyzing systems, testing, deployment, reliability, quantitative inference | Inspectable technical work with limitations; legal-tech interest does not establish engineering or statistical competence |
| Cross-functional roles | Coordinating decisions among specialist teams, resolving dependencies, translating constraints | Examples of changing a shared outcome without claiming expertise or authority the candidate did not possess |

For every row specify seniority and the organization's stage. A small company may combine several responsibilities and require independent execution; a larger one may narrow the role while increasing coordination demands. Neither is inherently better for a career changer. A broad title can conceal unsupported work; a narrow bridge role can build valuable, transferable evidence.

## 5. Law-to-technology and internationally educated candidates

Translate existing work without inflating it: statutory interpretation may support policy reasoning; interviewing clients may support stakeholder discovery; matter coordination may support some operations tasks. Each is a **transfer hypothesis** that needs a corresponding work sample and feedback. Do not treat “law graduate” as either a universal advantage or a permanent occupational boundary.

A bridge role is useful when it produces relevant work, credible observers, and contact with the target function. Name the missing link it is meant to build, the evidence expected after a defined interval, and an exit or review criterion. Prestige alone does not make a bridge effective. Repeatedly taking adjacent jobs without gaining the missing evidence is drift.

Education is one route among several. Separate a legally or institutionally required credential from structured learning, access to a recruiting channel, and a prestige signal. An LL.M., J.D., certificate, or additional degree can change some of these while leaving others unchanged. Compare the mechanism, cost, time, and feasible alternatives; load [LL.M. pathway](llm-pathway.md) for education, bar, or student-status mechanics. Verify current authorization with the appropriate government authority and qualified counsel; employer willingness and legal eligibility are separate questions.

## 6. Worked application — synthesis, not an outcome forecast

An internationally educated law graduate wants “AI policy at a technology company.” Keep at least three hypotheses open: policy research/analysis, policy operations, and product-adjacent governance work. First obtain an actual task description for each. A well-written legal essay may support research and interpretation while saying little about incident handling or product tradeoffs.

A bounded test could produce a public-information policy brief, an escalation workflow with difficult cases, and a short discussion with practitioners about which artifact resembles their work. Use permitted, nonconfidential material. Ask evaluators to identify missing task knowledge and unrealistic assumptions. Compare interest during the work, quality of feedback, and remaining entry barriers. These observations may support a direct application or a bridge; they do not establish that a company is hiring or that an immigration route is available.

## Decision rules

- Define a market with tasks, level, organization, and constraints before ranking titles.
- Separate capability, evidence, access, and legal eligibility; each calls for a different intervention.
- Treat mission attraction as a reason to investigate, not evidence of person–job fit.
- Use an organizational example to generate questions, never to infer a named firm's current process.
- Test whether education or a bridge changes the binding requirement before recommending the investment.
- End with the next discriminating action and what its possible results would change.
