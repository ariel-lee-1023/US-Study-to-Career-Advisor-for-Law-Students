# Recruitment, selection, and the strength of hiring evidence

**Sources:** Kang Yang Trevor Yu & Daniel M. Cable (eds.), *The Oxford Handbook of Recruitment* (supplied copyright 2014), especially Connerley ch. 3, Hausknecht ch. 4, Harold/Uggerslev/Kraichy ch. 5, Van Hoye ch. 14, Breaugh ch. 20, and Landis/Earnest/Allen ch. 23; Neal Schmitt (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of Personnel Assessment and Selection* (2012), especially chs. 6–7, 9–11, 15, 17, 20, and performance criteria; Atta Tarki, *Evidence-Based Recruiting* (2020), chs. 3–5, 7–11. Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, ch. 3 supplies an explicit operating-practice comparison in §6.
**Trigger:** interpreting recruiter contact, hiring stages, selection methods, interviews, assessments, employer requirements, rejection, or candidate evaluation; distinguishing research evidence from hiring practice.
**What this file is:** employer–candidate process analysis and limits on inference from hiring evidence. Recruitment and selection can occur in the same interaction, but answer different questions.

> **Vintage note.** These books support conceptual and methodological reasoning. Historical validity coefficients, rankings of tests, legal standards, assessment products, platform features, named employer policies, and interview loops are not current facts. This module supplies no contemporary ranking of selection methods. Retrieve current primary research for numerical or comparative scientific claims and official sources for legal requirements.

## 1. Recruitment is a continuing, two-sided process

Breaugh defines recruitment around making an opening visible, influencing application, maintaining candidate interest, and influencing offer acceptance. It does not stop when an application enters a system. Harold, Uggerslev & Kraichy likewise treat **job choice** as applying, remaining or withdrawing, and accepting or declining; attraction and intention are not interchangeable with observed decisions.

Trace both sides of each stage:

| Stage | Employer question | Candidate question | What an observation does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness / attraction | Are relevant people reached by the message and channel? | Is the work worth investigating? | Brand familiarity does not establish job fit |
| Application / initial contact | Who enters the pool, with what expectations and constraints? | Is there a credible route to consideration? | Recruiter outreach is not an offer or proof of qualification |
| Screening / assessment | What requirements and evidence determine advancement? | What is being measured and what can be learned about the employer? | Progress is conditional; one stage need not validate all capabilities |
| Continued participation | Can interest and trust be maintained? | Are time, treatment, alternatives and likely work acceptable? | Withdrawal is not necessarily an employer rejection |
| Offer / acceptance | Can an appropriate hire be closed on workable terms? | Does this specific offer satisfy constraints and needs? | Acceptance is not evidence of later performance or retention |

Breaugh's **recruitment process model** connects objectives → strategy → activities → intervening applicant variables → evaluation. Objectives include pre-hire quantities and deadlines, sought attributes and expectations, and post-hire performance or retention. Strategies ask whom to target, when to recruit, what message to convey, how to reach people, which recruiters to use, what a site visit should do, and what an offer should contain. For advising, reconstruct these as hypotheses about the employer's process; do not assume all employers plan coherently.

Attention, interest, perceived chance of an offer, alternatives, and accuracy of expectations mediate candidate behavior. A campaign attracting many people can still miss the relevant pool; a fast process can sacrifice information; a retention outcome does not by itself identify which channel caused it. Define the outcome before calling recruitment “effective.”

## 2. Interpret channels, messages, and applicant reactions

**Channels and source effects.** A campus channel can grant visibility; a referral can transmit information, familiarity, or an advocate's credibility; a public posting can reveal expressed requirements. Distinguish access, information quality, and prediction of performance. People self-select into channels, so better outcomes among referrals do not automatically identify a causal channel effect. Several employees repeating the same official account are not independent corroboration.

Van Hoye's **word-of-mouth integrative model** separates recipient, source, and organization characteristics. The **source–credibility framework** asks whether a speaker has relevant expertise and incentives; the **accessibility–diagnosticity model** distinguishes information readily available in memory from information useful for the judgment. Ask about the speaker's team, period, role, and direct experience. A friendly alumnus may know little about the target function; vivid stories need not be representative.

**Recruiter effects.** Connerley describes unresolved questions about their strength and mechanisms. Warmth, information and treatment can influence experience and attraction, but a pleasant recruiter is not a measure of future manager support, and a delayed reply has multiple possible explanations. Avoid fixed claims about how much recruiter behavior changes outcomes.

**Applicant reactions.** Hausknecht treats candidates' attitudes, emotions, and judgments about the process as consequential. Opportunity to demonstrate relevant ability, consistency, explanation, respect, and perceived fairness can affect participation and impressions. Separate “I disliked the assessment,” “the procedure was inconsistent,” “the score is unreliable,” and “the score cannot support the proposed inference.” These need different evidence.

**Realistic Job Previews (RJPs)** present both positive and negative job information. Use their logic to investigate routine work, hard days, constraints, and support, not only mission and perks. The literature discusses met expectations, perceived honesty, and turnover mechanisms; it does not justify assuming that candor necessarily drives desirable applicants away or guarantees retention. Ask for concrete examples and triangulate them.

## 3. The predictive inference is the central selection question

Sackett, Putka & McCloy's validity chapter asks whether evidence supports a **specified inference from a score for a proposed use**. “Is this a valid test?” is underspecified. Separate:

1. **Criterion:** What future behavior or outcome matters—task performance, learning, adaptation, teamwork, retention, safety? At what horizon and level?
2. **Construct:** What attribute is meant to explain or predict it—job knowledge, reasoning, conscientiousness, a specific skill? A criterion is not a construct, and a construct is not an instrument.
3. **Instrument and administration:** What tasks/questions, conditions, scoring rules, accommodations, and raters generated the evidence?
4. **Support for the inference:** What links the scores to the claimed attribute or outcome in the relevant setting? What does not transfer from the research population or job?
5. **Decision use:** Is it a threshold, ranking, complement to another measure, or merely one interviewer's impression? What costs follow from false positives and false negatives?

**Criterion-related evidence** examines relations with relevant outcomes; **content-oriented evidence** examines representation of the relevant domain; **construct-oriented evidence** examines what scores mean. These are sources of evidence for claims, not three stamps that make every use valid. A work sample resembling the job supports one argument, but does not automatically validate every score interpretation or long-term forecast.

**Reliability** concerns consistency of measurement. Consistent scoring of an irrelevant characteristic is still irrelevant. **Incremental validity** asks what a measure contributes beyond evidence already used; another interview repeating the same impression is not necessarily another independent signal.

Specify criterion quality. **Criterion deficiency** omits important work; **criterion contamination** introduces irrelevant influences. Sales or throughput may depend on resources and assignment, and supervisor ratings may include favoritism or administrative incentives. “Objective” is not synonymous with uncontaminated. Distinguish maximum performance on a short task from typical performance over time.

Meta-analysis can synthesize evidence but its meaning depends on included studies, measures, population, moderators and statistical corrections. **Range restriction**, sampling error and measurement error matter; corrected estimates are not an individual hiring probability. This module does not reproduce the books' historical coefficients or treat a past ordering of predictors as settled current science.

## 4. What common methods can and cannot show

| Method | Potential evidence | Important inference limit |
|---|---|---|
| Résumé, education, employment history | Exposure, credentials, claimed achievements, prior selection | Opportunity and institutional access affect the record; title or prestige is an indirect signal, not a work demonstration |
| Ability / knowledge measures | Performance on the sampled reasoning or knowledge domain | Need job and use relevance, administration quality, and fairness evidence; a result does not establish every performance dimension |
| Personality self-reports | Reported dispositions relevant to a specified hypothesis | Trait measures, situational behavior and job performance differ; self-presentation and context matter; do not diagnose a candidate or assign a career by personality type |
| Structured interview | Comparable evidence from job-related questions and defined rating criteria | Structure is multidimensional: questions, evaluation and combination of scores; the label alone is insufficient |
| Unstructured conversation | Context, reciprocal information, rapport and leads for further inquiry | Liking, similarity and inconsistent prompts can contaminate comparison; ease of conversation does not establish task skill |
| Work sample / assessment-center exercise | Actual behavior on sampled tasks or situations | Task coverage, prior exposure, resources, fidelity, rater quality and scoring still matter; one exercise cannot prove overall future success |
| Situational judgment test (SJT) | Responses to described work dilemmas, often selecting an option | A low-fidelity simulation is not the same as performing the work; intended constructs and scoring require evidence |
| References | Contextual observations from people with relevant exposure | Selection of referees, incentives, recall and opportunity to observe constrain inference; confident praise is not standardized evidence |

The Schmitt interview chapter reports stronger reliability and criterion-related validity for structured interviews in its reviewed literature, while also preserving debate about the flexibility and informational uses of less structured interviews. It does not warrant treating informal dialogue as useless or equally strong for every prediction. Keep exploration and recruiting conversation distinct from comparable scoring of job requirements.

The simulations chapter distinguishes **high-fidelity simulations** (work samples and assessment-center exercises) from **low-fidelity simulations** (such as SJTs). Apparent realism does not settle construct interpretation, incremental value, susceptibility to coaching, subgroup effects, or cross-cultural transport. For a candidate, prepare the underlying tasks and explain assumptions; do not infer the employer's hidden rubric from format alone.

## 5. Fairness and rejection require separate questions

Distinguish **predictive bias** (systematic over- or underprediction for a group), differences in selection rates, measurement problems, applicant perceptions of fairness, and legal compliance. None is a complete substitute for the others. Relevant credentials or test scores do not prove that a whole process is fair; unequal outcomes alone do not identify a particular cause. A legal conclusion requires current law and jurisdiction-specific analysis.

For an internationally educated candidate, language, unfamiliar examples, credential recognition, work authorization and access to opportunities can enter at different stages. Identify which is directly evidenced. Do not infer a national-origin barrier, individual inability, or discrimination from one unexplained rejection, and do not dismiss a documented procedural problem as mere disappointment.

A rejection is an outcome of an unknown comparison and decision process. Other candidates, role changes, budget, internal applicants, access, performance evidence, perceived fit and social evaluation are competing explanations. Request actionable feedback when feasible; otherwise mark causal claims as unresolved. Do not invent an employer's reason.

## 6. Operational hiring discipline and genuine disagreements

Tarki supplies practical process recommendations: define success before evaluating candidates; specify questions and strong/weak evidence; use anchored ratings; probe claims; and learn from outcomes. **Calibrated Independent Estimates (CIE)** asks interviewers to form and record their own ratings and arguments before the group discussion, then combine and scrutinize them. Independence is a process goal, not a guarantee merely because several people participated.

An operational evidence packet can contain the requirement, observed behavior or artifact, scoring rule, counterevidence, uncertainty, and follow-up needed. Candidate-side use: prepare true examples of task, action, result, limitations, and learning; do not fabricate experience or optimize for a guessed personality profile.

Three tensions must remain visible:

- **Practice versus validation.** Tarki's recommendations and company stories suggest operating choices. Schmitt's research framework asks what inference the resulting data actually support. A numerical scorecard can make an unsupported judgment look precise; a widely copied process is not thereby validated.
- **Veto power.** Tarki ch. 11 argues against individual interviewer vetoes; Hughes Johnson ch. 3 describes a rare veto within Stripe's consultative process. These differ in safeguards, decision rights, and assumptions about error. Ask which concern the dissent identifies, whether evidence is job-related, and who is accountable. Neither historical example establishes a universal rule or current company practice.
- **Formal measurement versus social influence.** Standardized evidence can improve comparison while informal gatekeeping still shapes who is seen and believed. Rivera's account explains such mechanisms in a bounded setting; it does not establish their predictive validity. Load [Fit and gatekeeping](fit-and-gatekeeping.md) when this distinction matters.

## Decision rules

- Identify the stage and the outcome before interpreting a recruiting event.
- Ask “which construct, measured how, to predict what?” before accepting a selection claim.
- Keep applicant attraction, performance prediction, practical decision rules and fairness distinct.
- Prefer job-relevant, comparable evidence while retaining instrument and context limitations.
- Do not convert rejection, a test result, a referral, or interview warmth into a global judgment of merit.
- Verify contemporary coefficients, employer practices and legal rules; label historical examples as historical.
